What we file

Small Company Accounts Go to Companies House and the Tax Return Goes to HMRC

Filing small company accounts is not one submission. We send the statutory accounts to Companies House and the Company Tax Return to HMRC, because the two bodies enforce two separate statutory duties on the same company.

Companies House receives the statutory accounts, tagged in iXBRL. A company in the small companies regime does not have to deliver a copy of the directors' report or the profit and loss account, which is why most small company filings show a balance sheet and notes only. Your company still delivers a confirmation statement to Companies House, and that filing sits separately from the accounts.

HMRC receives the Company Tax Return. The CT600 arrives with a corporation tax computation and the accounts attached in iXBRL, and together they show HMRC how the tax figure was reached.

The two deadlines are not the same deadline, and the payment date sits between them. Accounts reach Companies House at 9 months. The CT600 reaches HMRC at 12 months. The Corporation Tax itself is payable at 9 months and one day, roughly three months before the return that calculates it. We work to all three dates together.

Qualification

Does Your Company File Small Company Accounts or Micro-Entity Accounts?

Your company files whichever set of accounts matches the size regime it falls into, and that regime decides which reporting standard applies. A micro-entity reports under FRS 105 and files a shortened balance sheet. A small company that exceeds the micro-entity thresholds reports under FRS 102 Section 1A and files a fuller set. You must meet at least two of the three conditions in a band to qualify for it.

Filing Small Company Accounts Under FRS 102 Section 1A

Your company qualifies as small for an accounting period beginning on or after 6 April 2025 when it meets at least two of three conditions: turnover of not more than £15 million, a balance sheet total of not more than £7.5 million, and not more than 50 employees on average.

A company that meets that test files under the small companies regime and takes the disclosure exemptions the regime allows. Accounts are prepared under FRS 102 Section 1A, which sets reduced disclosure requirements compared with full FRS 102 while requiring more than FRS 105.

The same size test also governs audit. A company that meets two of those three conditions is eligible for audit exemption, so most small companies file unaudited accounts and include the audit exemption statement on the balance sheet.

The thresholds moved on 6 April 2025. They rose from £10.2 million turnover and £5.1 million balance sheet total. Many more companies now qualify as small than qualified under the previous figures, and a good deal of published guidance still quotes the old ones.

Filing Micro-Entity Accounts Under FRS 105

Your company qualifies as a micro-entity when it meets at least two of three conditions: turnover of not more than £1 million, a balance sheet total of not more than £500,000, and not more than 10 employees on average.

A micro-entity prepares its accounts under FRS 105 and delivers a shortened balance sheet to Companies House. FRS 105 permits the simplest form of accounts available to a UK limited company, and it does not require many of the disclosures FRS 102 Section 1A demands.

A company that qualifies as a micro-entity may still choose to prepare small company accounts instead. Directors sometimes make that choice when a lender, an investor or a prospective buyer expects a fuller set of figures. Companies That Cannot Use the Small Companies Regime

Some companies are excluded from the small companies regime regardless of their size, and some are excluded from FRS 105 specifically. The exclusions reach categories such as public companies, certain financial services entities, and members of some groups.

Your accountant checks those exclusions before relying on the regime, because a company that files under a regime it is not entitled to use has not discharged its duty.

Account formats

Statutory, Abridged and Filleted Accounts Decide What the Public Register Shows

Three terms describe what a small company can deliver to Companies House, and they are routinely confused. The distinction decides how much of your financial position appears on the public record.

Statutory accounts

Statutory accounts are the annual accounts a company must prepare under the Companies Act 2006. A full set includes a balance sheet, a profit and loss account, notes to the accounts and, where one applies, a directors' report. The accounts your members receive and the accounts you deliver to Companies House are not necessarily the same document.

Abridged accounts

Abridged accounts are a shortened form of the accounts themselves, prepared with less detail in the balance sheet and profit and loss account. Abridgement changes what is prepared, not merely what is filed. [DATA NEEDED: verify whether all members must agree to abridgement, and the mechanism for obtaining that agreement]

Filleted accounts

Filleted accounts are a full set of accounts from which the profit and loss account and the directors' report have been withheld on filing. Filleting is an informal nickname rather than a statutory term. Section 444 of the Companies Act 2006 provides that a company subject to the small companies regime may also deliver to the registrar a copy of its profit and loss account and a copy of its directors' report, and it is that permissive "may" that creates the option to withhold them.

Where either document is withheld, the balance sheet must carry a statement that the company's annual accounts and reports have been delivered in accordance with the provisions applicable to companies subject to the small companies regime.

FormatWhat it changesWhat reaches the public register

Abbreviated accounts are a fourth term you may still encounter. They were withdrawn and are no longer a filing option, although older guidance across the web continues to describe them.

What filleting hides, and who can still see your figures

Withholding your profit and loss account keeps your turnover and profit off the public register, which is why most small companies file this way. It does not make those figures invisible everywhere. Your full accounts still go to HMRC with your CT600, your members still receive the complete set, and a lender or prospective buyer conducting due diligence will normally ask for the full accounts directly. Filleting is a privacy decision with a financing trade-off, and it is worth taking deliberately rather than by default. Two of these options end on 1 April 2028. From that date small companies must deliver a profit and loss account to Companies House, and micro-entities must prepare and deliver one with the option to opt out of publishing it on the public register. From the same date companies will no longer be able to file abridged accounts. If privacy over your trading figures is part of why you file the way you do, that position changes, and it is worth planning for rather than discovering.

Corporation Tax

Small Company Accounts Feed the Corporation Tax Your Company Pays

The accounts and the tax return are not independent documents. The profit your accounts report is the starting point for the corporation tax computation, and that computation produces the figure your company pays.

Corporation Tax has run at two rates since 1 April 2023. The main rate is 25% and it applies to profits over £250,000. The small profits rate is 19% and it applies to profits of £50,000 or less. Between those two figures a company claims Marginal Relief, which tapers the effective rate between the two.

Two adjustments catch companies out. Both thresholds are proportionately reduced for a short accounting period, and both are divided by the total number of associated companies. A director who runs three companies therefore reaches the main rate far earlier than a director who runs one.

Corporation Tax rates in force from 1 April 2023
Profit for the periodRate that applies
£50,000 or less19% small profits rate
Between £50,000 and £250,000Marginal Relief applies
Over £250,00025% main rate

Claiming Marginal Relief correctly is the work. A company sitting between the two thresholds pays more than 19% and less than 25%, and the associated companies count moves that boundary. Your accountant checks the count before the computation is prepared.

The tax figure matters. The date it falls due matters just as much.

Deadlines and penalties

Late Small Company Accounts Expose the Company to Penalties on Two Fronts

Companies House and HMRC each run their own penalty regime, and both regimes apply whether or not any tax falls due. A company that files its accounts on time but its tax return late still collects a penalty.

The Companies House deadline runs from the accounting reference date. Unless you are filing your company's first accounts, the time normally allowed for delivering accounts to Companies House is 9 months from the accounting reference date for a private company. First accounts covering a period of more than 12 months must reach Companies House within 21 months of the date of incorporation for private companies, or 3 months from the accounting reference date, whichever is longer.

Failure to deliver accounts on time is a criminal offence. The law additionally imposes a penalty for late filing of accounts on the company, and the amount of that penalty depends on how late the accounts arrive.

Directors ask the same six clusters of questions before they file small company accounts. Each tab below answers one cluster.

Do I file the same accounts with Companies House and HMRC?

No. Companies House receives your statutory accounts, and HMRC receives your Company Tax Return with the computation and the accounts attached in iXBRL. The two submissions contain different documents and fall due on different dates.

Who files the accounts, you or the director?

We file them. We send the accounts and tax return to you for your approval, explain what tax is owed, answer your questions, and then file directly with HMRC and Companies House.

What is iXBRL and why do my accounts need it?

iXBRL is the machine-readable format required for filed accounts. Tagging attaches a standard label to each figure so the receiving system can read it automatically. We tag your accounts as part of the fixed fee, so you do not need filing software of your own.

What counts as a small company?

For accounting periods beginning on or after 6 April 2025, a company qualifies as small by meeting at least two of three conditions: turnover of not more than £15 million, a balance sheet total of not more than £7.5 million, and not more than 50 employees on average.

What counts as a micro-entity?

For accounting periods beginning on or after 6 April 2025, a company qualifies as a micro-entity by meeting at least two of three conditions: turnover of not more than £1 million, a balance sheet total of not more than £500,000, and not more than 10 employees on average.

What is FRS 102 Section 1A?

FRS 102 Section 1A is the reporting standard that applies to small companies that are not micro-entities. It sets reduced disclosure requirements compared with full FRS 102, while requiring more than FRS 105.

What is FRS 105?

FRS 105 is the reporting standard for micro-entities. It permits the simplest form of accounts available to a UK limited company and does not require many of the disclosures FRS 102 Section 1A demands.

When must small company accounts reach Companies House?

Companies House normally allows 9 months from the accounting reference date for a private company. First accounts covering more than 12 months must arrive within 21 months of the date of incorporation, or 3 months from the accounting reference date, whichever is longer.

When is the Company Tax Return due, and when is the tax due?

The return is due 12 months after the end of the accounting period. The Corporation Tax itself is usually payable 9 months and one day after the end of the period, which is roughly three months before the return that calculates it.

What does Companies House charge for late accounts?

Four bands apply to private companies and LLPs: £150 for not more than 1 month, £375 for more than 1 month but not more than 3 months, £750 for more than 3 months but not more than 6 months, and £1,500 for more than 6 months. The penalty doubles where accounts are filed late in 2 successive financial years.

What does HMRC charge for a late company tax return?

HMRC charges £200 at 1 day late, a further £200 at 3 months, 10% of the unpaid tax at 6 months, and a further 10% at 12 months. A return filed late 3 times in a row raises each £200 penalty to £1,000.

Does a small company need an audit?

Most do not. For accounting periods beginning on or after 6 April 2025, a company is eligible for audit exemption by meeting at least two of three conditions: turnover of not more than £15 million, a balance sheet total of not more than £7.5 million, and not more than 50 employees on average. Some companies are excluded from the exemption regardless of size.

What is the difference between abridged and filleted accounts?

Abridgement changes what is prepared, by reducing the detail in the balance sheet and profit and loss account. Filleting changes only what is filed, by withholding the profit and loss account and directors' report from the public register while the full set still exists.

Can I keep my turnover off the public register?

At present, yes. A company in the small companies regime does not have to deliver its profit and loss account to Companies House. That changes on 1 April 2028, when small companies must deliver a profit and loss account and abridged accounts cease to be a filing option.
