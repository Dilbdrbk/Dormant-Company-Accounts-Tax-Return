What goes public

Abridged, Filleted, or Full: What Actually Goes on the Public Record

Small companies have three filing routes. Abridged and filleted get used interchangeably all over the web, and they're not the same thing.

Filleted accounts

  • What almost every small company actually files
  • Full accounts go to your members and HMRC; the filed copy leaves out the profit and loss account and usually the directors' report
  • No shareholder consent needed
  • The balance sheet has to state that the accounts were delivered under the small companies regime, and it must disclose that the profit and loss account hasn't been filed
  • Your turnover and profit stay off the public register

Abridged accounts

  • Rarer, and a different mechanism entirely
  • A genuinely simpler set of accounts prepared for the members, not just a trimmed filing copy
  • Every member has to agree, under section 444(2A) of the Companies Act 2006
  • The abridged profit and loss account can start at gross profit, leaving out turnover and cost of sales. The abridged balance sheet drops the numbered items, so the debtors and creditors notes can go
  • Abridged accounts can then be filleted as well

Worth knowing before you assume filleting hides everything: the average employee numbers note is generally still filed, on the reading that it isn't a profit and loss account note. So a filleted set still puts your headcount on the public register. And abridged accounts disappear altogether in April 2028, so any company relying on them needs a different plan. Know the basics

What Qualifies as a Small Company?

Your company is small if it meets any two of these three conditions. It does not need to meet all three.

Turnover of £15 million or less

£7.5 million or less on the balance sheet

50 employees or less

How the two-year rule works

In your first financial year, meeting the limits in that year is enough. After that, gaining or losing small company status only counts if it happens in two consecutive financial years. Turnover is adjusted in proportion for any period that isn't a full twelve months, and the employee figure is a monthly average across the year rather than a headcount on your year-end date.

Small company or micro-entity?

Every micro-entity is also small, so the real question is which regime you report under. A small company that isn't a micro-entity uses FRS 102 Section 1A. A micro-entity can use FRS 105, which is simpler again. The difference is bigger than the thresholds suggest.

Section 1A changes what you disclose, not how you measure. Recognition and measurement follow full FRS 102. FRS 105 genuinely simplifies the measurement basis. Moving up from FRS 105 to Section 1A is more work than crossing a threshold sounds, and it needs your transition-date balances restated.

Do small companies need an audit?

No, in most cases. The audit exemption size test tracks the small company test, so a company inside these limits is normally exempt. Three things override that. Your articles of association can require an audit. Shareholders holding at least 10% of shares, by number or value, can demand one in writing, sent to your registered office at least a month before your financial year ends. And membership of a group or a regulated sector can remove the exemption regardless of size. An audit exemption statement goes on your balance sheet either way.

Size isn't the only test. A company in an ineligible group, or operating in a regulated sector such as insurance or banking, can't use the small companies regime whatever its numbers. Subsidiaries have a separate route under section 479A, which needs a parent company guarantee. And if your company pays dividends, it generally needs a statement of changes in equity for the accounts to give a true and fair view, even though that statement never gets filed at Companies House.

Filing Deadlines

  • Annual accounts9 months after your accounting reference date
  • First accounts21 months after incorporation, or 3 months after the ARD, whichever is longer
  • Corporation Tax payment9 months and 1 day after your period ends
  • CT600 tax return12 months after your period ends
  • Confirmation statementWithin 14 days of your review period ending

Your tax is due three months before your tax return is. That ordering catches people out every year.

Late Filing Penalties

  • Up to 1 month late£150
  • 1–3 months late£375
  • 3–6 months late£750
  • Over 6 months late£1,500

Those are Companies House penalties for late accounts, and they double if you file late two years running. HMRC charges separately for a late CT600: £200 at one day, another £200 at three months, then 10% of unpaid tax at six months and another 10% at twelve. Three late returns in a row raise the £200 charges to £1,000 each. Both regulators can penalise the same year-end.

Two changes to know

What's Changing for Small Company Accounts

One is already in force and affects the accounts you're preparing now. The other lands in April 2028.

The FRS 102 changes, already in force

The FRC completed its Periodic Review 2024 in March 2024, with a principal effective date of 1 January 2026. If your accounting period began on or after that date, the revised standard applies. A 31 December year end means the accounts for the year ending 31 December 2026. A 31 March year end means the period starting 1 April 2026.

  • Most leases come onto the balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a matching lease liability. The operating and finance lease split is gone for lessees. Short-term leases of twelve months or less and low-value assets are exempt.
  • Lease costs become depreciation and interest rather than rent. That moves EBITDA, gearing and net debt without your cash flows changing at all, so it's worth reading your bank covenants before your year end rather than after it.
  • Revenue moves to a five-step recognition model, which can change when revenue is recognised on contracts with several elements.
  • Section 1A picks up expanded related party disclosures, plus new disclosure requirements covering leases, revenue, tax and going concern.
  • Transition uses a modified retrospective approach: prior-year figures aren't restated, and a cumulative adjustment goes to opening retained earnings.
The lease changes apply to FRS 102, including Section 1A, but not to FRS 105. A micro-entity keeps operating leases off its balance sheet. The five-step revenue model does reach FRS 105, in simplified form, so micro-entities aren't untouched. This is the clearest practical difference between the two regimes right now.

Honest scoping: a company with no leases and straightforward sales will barely notice any of this. A company with premises, vehicles or equipment on lease has real work to do, and a single office lease is usually enough to matter.

What changes at Companies House in April 2028

Companies House confirmed this on 9 June 2026. Most guidance still online gives the date as April 2027, or says the reforms were paused with no date. Both are out of date.

  • You'll file a profit and loss account with Companies House, with the option to opt out of publishing it on the public register. The opt-out mechanism hasn't been published yet.
  • Abridged accounts are removed entirely.
  • All accounts must be filed through commercial software in iXBRL format, whether you file yourself or use an accountant. Companies House web and paper routes close for accounts, though web filing stays open for confirmation statements and director updates.
  • Claiming audit exemption will need a strengthened eligibility statement.
  • The component parts of your accounts will have to be filed together.
  • Shortening your accounting reference period gets limited, closing a route some companies use to buy filing time.
The practical read for a small company: filleting has a shelf life. From April 2028 the profit and loss account gets filed, and privacy comes from the opt-out rather than from leaving it out. Every company gets one full accounting year plus nine months to prepare, and Micro Filer already files through commercial software.

Common Questions

Is a small company the same as a micro-entity?+

No. Every micro-entity is also a small company, but not every small company is a micro-entity. A micro-entity meets two of three lower limits: turnover of £1 million or less, £500,000 or less on the balance sheet, and 10 employees or less. Small companies sit inside much larger limits and report under FRS 102 Section 1A rather than FRS 105.

What's the difference between abridged and filleted accounts?+

Filleted means filing your accounts without the profit and loss account and usually without the directors' report. No shareholder consent is needed and it's what most small companies do. Abridged means preparing a genuinely simpler set of accounts for your members, which needs every member to agree. The two get used interchangeably online, but abridging changes the accounts themselves while filleting only changes what gets filed.

Do I have to file my profit and loss account?+

Not at the moment. A small company can file without it, and most do. From April 2028 the profit and loss account has to be filed, but with an option to opt out of publishing it on the public register. Companies House hasn't published how the opt-out will work yet.

Do small companies need an audit?+

No, in most cases, since the audit exemption size test tracks the small company test. Your articles of association can require one, shareholders holding at least 10% of shares can demand one in writing, and group or regulated-sector membership can remove the exemption whatever your size. An audit exemption statement goes on your balance sheet either way.

Will the FRS 102 lease changes affect my company?+

Only if you lease something. If you rent premises, vehicles or equipment on anything longer than twelve months, most of those leases now come onto your balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability. Short-term and low-value leases are exempt. If you own everything you use, nothing changes on this front.

Can I use FRS 105 instead if I qualify as a micro-entity?+

Yes, and some companies should. FRS 105 is simpler, keeps operating leases off the balance sheet, and puts less on the public register. It also can't recognise defined benefit pension balances and gives you fewer measurement options, so it isn't automatically the better choice. FRS 105 vs FRS 102 works through the decision.
