What we file

Dormant Company Accounts and the Company Tax Return Go to Two Different Places

Filing dormant company accounts is not one submission. We send the accounts to Companies House and the Company Tax Return to HMRC, because the two bodies enforce two separate statutory duties on the same company.

Companies House receives the annual accounts. A dormant company that qualifies as small does not have to include an auditor's report with those accounts, because section 480 of the Companies Act 2006 exempts a company that has been dormant since its formation, or dormant since the end of the previous financial year, from the audit requirement. Your company still delivers a confirmation statement to Companies House, and that filing sits separately from the accounts.

HMRC receives the Company Tax Return. A company that has already filed a Company Tax Return, or that has received a notice to deliver one, must still file online, and that return shows HMRC the company was dormant for the period. In practice the submission is a normal CT600 carrying zero in every figure, known as a nil return, and no separate nil-return form exists.

The two dormancy tests are not the same test. Dormant for Corporation Tax and dormant for Companies House measure different things, and a company can meet one and fail the other. We check both tests against your company before we submit anything.

Qualification

Does Your Company Qualify to File Dormant Company Accounts?

A company qualifies to file dormant company accounts when it passes the dormancy test that the relevant body applies. Four routes lead to that answer, and your accountant works through each one before we prepare your accounts.

Filing Dormant Company Accounts After a Year With No Significant Transaction

Companies House treats your company as dormant when it had no significant transaction in the financial year. Section 1169 of the Companies Act 2006 defines a significant accounting transaction as a transaction that section 386 requires the company to enter in its accounting records. That statutory wording, not a rule of thumb, sets the boundary.

The Act disregards a short list of payments when it applies that test. Those disregarded items are the shares taken by the subscribers to the memorandum on formation, the registrar's fee for a change of name, the registrar's fee for re-registration, the registrar's fee for registering a confirmation statement, and a penalty under section 453 for failing to file accounts. Put plainly, filing fees paid to Companies House, late filing penalties, and money paid for shares at incorporation all leave dormancy intact.

A company that passes this test files dormant accounts rather than full trading accounts. That route protects the director from preparing figures the company does not have, and it keeps the public record accurate.

A worked example makes the test concrete. A company incorporated in March 2025 issues 100 £1 shares to its single subscriber, pays a Companies House fee to register its confirmation statement, and makes no other payment. Both items sit on the disregard list, so the company remains dormant for Companies House and files dormant accounts for that year.

Filing a Nil Company Tax Return When the Company Is Dormant for Corporation Tax

HMRC applies its own dormancy test, and that test turns on income rather than on accounting records. A company counts as dormant for Corporation Tax when it has stopped trading and has no other income, for example investments. The same treatment reaches a new limited company that has not started trading, an unincorporated association or club owing less than £100 Corporation Tax, and a flat management company.

The definition of trading runs wider than most directors expect. Trading includes buying, selling, renting property, advertising, employing someone or getting interest. A single bank interest credit therefore fails the Corporation Tax dormancy test even though the company sold nothing.

A notice from HMRC overrides the company's own view. A company that has already filed a Company Tax Return, or that has received a notice to deliver one, must still file online, and the return shows HMRC the company was dormant for the period.

One risk deserves attention here. A director who assumes dormancy without telling HMRC can miss a notice to deliver and expose the company to an automatic penalty. Where HMRC has confirmed the company is dormant and has issued no notice, no return falls due. That confirmation is the trigger, not your own assumption that nothing happened.

Filing Dormant Company Accounts for a Company That Has Never Traded

A company that has been dormant since its formation sits in the cleanest position under the Companies Act. Section 480(1)(a) of the Companies Act 2006 exempts such a company from the audit requirement for that financial year outright. Section 480(2) attaches two conditions to the alternative route, and those conditions concern the small companies regime and group accounts.

Those conditions are that the company is entitled to prepare its individual accounts under the small companies regime in sections 381 to 384, or would be entitled but for having been a public company or a member of an ineligible group, and that the company is not required to prepare group accounts for that year. The practical result is straightforward: a dormant company that qualifies as small does not need an audit.

Section 480 takes effect subject to three other provisions, namely section 475 on the statements required in the balance sheet, section 476 on the right of members to require an audit, and section 481 on companies excluded from the dormant companies exemption. We check those exclusions before we rely on the exemption.

This case recurs often. A director incorporates a company to reserve a trading name, opens no bank account, and issues one share. That company has been dormant since formation, so section 480(1)(a) applies and we file dormant accounts with no auditor's report.

Deadlines and penalties

Late Dormant Company Accounts Expose the Company to Penalties on Two Fronts

A dormant company owes no Corporation Tax in most periods, yet it still fails its duties if it files late. Companies House and HMRC each run their own penalty regime, and both regimes apply whether or not any tax falls due.

The Companies House deadline runs from the accounting reference date. Unless you are filing your company's first accounts, the time normally allowed for delivering accounts to Companies House is 9 months from the accounting reference date for a private company. First accounts covering a period of more than 12 months must reach Companies House within 21 months of the date of incorporation for private companies, or 3 months from the accounting reference date, whichever is longer.

Failure to deliver accounts on time is a criminal offence. The law additionally imposes a penalty for late filing of accounts on the company, and the amount of that penalty depends on how late the accounts arrive.

Does filing dormant accounts also cover the confirmation statement?

The confirmation statement is a separate Companies House filing. Even a dormant company must submit a confirmation statement, previously called the annual return, as well as annual accounts. [DATA NEEDED: confirm whether the £285 fee includes filing the confirmation statement]

Do dormant accounts need an auditor's report?

Usually no. A dormant company that qualifies as small does not need an audit. Section 480 of the Companies Act 2006 sets the conditions, and it takes effect subject to section 476, which preserves the right of members to require an audit.

What if the company used accounting software, or used none at all?

Either position works. We integrate with a range of accounting software packages, and if you use none of them that is also fine.

What does dormant actually mean in law?

Section 1169 of the Companies Act 2006 provides that a company is dormant during any period in which it has no significant accounting transaction. A significant accounting transaction means a transaction that section 386 requires the company to enter in its accounting records.

Which payments do not break dormancy?

Three in everyday terms: filing fees paid to Companies House, penalties for late filing of accounts, and money paid for shares when the company was incorporated. Section 1169 adds the registrar's fees for a change of name, for re-registration, and for registering a confirmation statement.

Can a company be dormant for one body and not the other?

Yes. Dormant for Corporation Tax and dormant for Companies House measure different things, and a company can meet one and fail the other. We check both tests separately rather than assuming.

Does bank interest stop a company being dormant for Corporation Tax?

Yes. Trading for this test includes buying, selling, renting property, advertising, employing someone or getting interest. Interest received is therefore income, and it breaks Corporation Tax dormancy.

When must dormant accounts reach Companies House?

Companies House normally allows 9 months from the accounting reference date for a private company. First accounts covering more than 12 months must arrive within 21 months of the date of incorporation, or 3 months from the accounting reference date, whichever is longer.

What does Companies House charge for late accounts?

Four bands apply to private companies and LLPs: £150 for not more than 1 month, £375 for more than 1 month but not more than 3 months, £750 for more than 3 months but not more than 6 months, and £1,500 for more than 6 months. What does HMRC charge for a late company tax return?

HMRC charges £200 at 1 day late, a further £200 at 3 months, 10% of the unpaid tax at 6 months, and a further 10% at 12 months. A return filed late 3 times in a row raises each £200 penalty to £1,000.

Is late filing more than a financial matter?

What is a nil return?
