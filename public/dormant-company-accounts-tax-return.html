What Micro Filer files

Dormant Company Accounts and the Company Tax Return Go to Two Different Places

Filing dormant company accounts is not one submission. Micro Filer sends the accounts to Companies House and the Company Tax Return to HMRC, because the two bodies enforce two separate statutory duties on the same company.

Companies House receives the annual accounts. A dormant company that qualifies as small does not have to include an auditor's report with those accounts, because section 480 of the Companies Act 2006 exempts a company that has been dormant since its formation, or dormant since the end of the previous financial year, from the audit requirement. Your company still delivers a confirmation statement to Companies House, and that filing is separate from the accounts.

HMRC receives the Company Tax Return. GOV.UK states that a company which has already filed a Company Tax Return, or which has received a notice to deliver one, must still file online, and that this return shows HMRC the company is dormant for the period. Micro Filer's own guidance describes that submission as a normal CT600 with every figure at zero, known as a nil return, and confirms that no separate nil-return form exists.

The two dormancy tests are not the same test. Micro Filer's guidance states that dormant for Corporation Tax and dormant for Companies House are two different tests, and that a company can meet one and not the other. Micro Filer checks both tests against your company before it files anything.

Qualification

Does Your Company Qualify to File Dormant Company Accounts?

A company qualifies to file dormant company accounts when it passes the dormancy test that the relevant body applies. Four routes lead to that answer, and Micro Filer works through each one before it prepares your filing.

Filing Dormant Company Accounts After a Year With No Significant Transaction

Companies House treats your company as dormant when it had no significant transaction in the financial year. Section 1169 of the Companies Act 2006 defines a significant accounting transaction as a transaction that section 386 requires the company to enter in its accounting records. That statutory wording, not a rule of thumb, sets the boundary.

The Act disregards a short list of payments when it applies the test. Those disregarded items are the shares taken by the subscribers to the memorandum on formation, the registrar's fee for a change of name, the registrar's fee for re-registration, the registrar's fee for registering a confirmation statement, and a penalty under section 453 for failing to file accounts. GOV.UK gives the same list in plain terms as filing fees paid to Companies House, penalties for late filing of accounts, and money paid for shares when the company was incorporated.

A company that passes this test files dormant accounts rather than full trading accounts. That route protects the director from preparing figures the company does not have, and it keeps the public record accurate.

A worked example makes the test concrete. A company incorporated in March 2025 issues 100 £1 shares to its single subscriber, pays a Companies House fee to register its confirmation statement, and makes no other payment. Both items sit on the disregard list, so the company remains dormant for Companies House and files dormant accounts for that year.

Filing a Nil Company Tax Return When the Company Is Dormant for Corporation Tax

HMRC applies its own dormancy test, and that test turns on income rather than on accounting records. GOV.UK states that a company is usually dormant for Corporation Tax when it has stopped trading and has no other income, for example investments. GOV.UK also treats a new limited company that has not started trading, an unincorporated association or club owing less than £100 Corporation Tax, and a flat management company as dormant for Corporation Tax.

The definition of trading is wider than most directors expect. GOV.UK states that trading includes buying, selling, renting property, advertising, employing someone or getting interest. A single bank interest credit therefore fails the Corporation Tax dormancy test even though the company sold nothing.

A notice from HMRC overrides the company's own view. GOV.UK states that a company which has already filed a Company Tax Return, or which has received a notice to deliver one, must still file online, and that the return shows HMRC the company is dormant for the period.

One risk deserves attention here. A director who assumes dormancy without telling HMRC can miss a notice to deliver and expose the company to an automatic penalty. Micro Filer's guidance states that if HMRC has confirmed your company is dormant and has not issued a notice, you do not need to file, and that the confirmation is the trigger, not your own assumption that nothing happened.

Filing Dormant Company Accounts for a Company That Has Never Traded

A company that has been dormant since its formation sits in the cleanest position under the Companies Act. Section 480(1)(a) of the Companies Act 2006 exempts such a company from the audit requirement for that financial year outright. Section 480(2) attaches two conditions to the alternative route, and those conditions concern the small companies regime and group accounts.

Those conditions are that the company is entitled to prepare its individual accounts under the small companies regime in sections 381 to 384, or would be entitled but for having been a public company or a member of an ineligible group, and that the company is not required to prepare group accounts for that year. GOV.UK states the practical result plainly: dormant companies that qualify as small do not need to be audited.

Section 480 takes effect subject to three other provisions, namely section 475 on the statements required in the balance sheet, section 476 on the right of members to require an audit, and section 481 on companies excluded from the dormant companies exemption. Micro Filer checks those exclusions before it relies on the exemption.

This case recurs often. A director incorporates a company to reserve a trading name, opens no bank account, and issues one share. That company has been dormant since formation, so section 480(1)(a) applies and Micro Filer files dormant accounts with no auditor's report.

Deadlines and penalties

Late Dormant Company Accounts Expose the Company to Penalties on Two Fronts

A dormant company owes no Corporation Tax in most periods, yet it still fails its duties if it files late. Companies House and HMRC each run their own penalty regime, and both regimes apply whether or not any tax is due.

The Companies House deadline runs from the accounting reference date. Micro Filer's guidance states that unless you are filing your company's first accounts, the time normally allowed for delivering accounts to Companies House is 9 months from the accounting reference date for a private company. First accounts covering a period of more than 12 months must reach Companies House within 21 months of the date of incorporation for private companies, or 3 months from the accounting reference date, whichever is longer.

Failure to deliver accounts on time is a criminal offence. Micro Filer's guidance states that the law additionally imposes a penalty for late filing of accounts on the company, and that the amount of the penalty depends on how late the accounts arrive.

What does dormant actually mean in law?

Section 1169 of the Companies Act 2006 states that a company is dormant during any period in which it has no significant accounting transaction. A significant accounting transaction means a transaction that section 386 requires the company to enter in its accounting records.

GOV.UK lists three: filing fees paid to Companies House, penalties for late filing of accounts, and money paid for shares when the company was incorporated. Section 1169 adds the registrar's fees for a change of name, for re-registration, and for registering a confirmation statement.
